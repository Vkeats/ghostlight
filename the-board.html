What it did

Built for the worst morning of the week

  • One-click calling sheetsfrom 5–45 minutes of setup to a single action, any show size
  • Bundled SMS dispatchoffers, confirmations, and recalls with inline response buttons
  • Two-way legacy syncidempotent push/pull against the incumbent steward system — zero date/time mismatches
  • Skill-matrix exportsa venue's 31 skill lists: an hour of compiling, done in one click
  • Per-skill PPE defaultswardrobe doesn't get hard-hat notices; electrics always bring the wrench
  • Availability & conflict awarenessrecurring unavailability synced, time-aware conflict detection on export

Shipped in one month of the pilot, solo, after hours: 15 features, 32 bug fixes, 2 performance improvements.

The arc

Built, shipped, operated, retired

The Board began as an internal tool: a working dispatcher automating his own worst mornings. It grew into a 90-day paid pilot with his local — invoiced monthly, every invoice paid within a day.

One person was the operator and the developer at the same time. That is the reason the fill rate was 95.8%. It is not the reason the pilot didn't continue.

The pitch called it dispatch automation. To the dispatch team — union members themselves — a tool that let one dispatcher do the work of several sounded like a tool that made the others unnecessary, and their union asked for a pause. That reading was fair, and the framing was wrong. The Board never dispatched anyone on its own; every offer went out because a dispatcher decided it should. It was augmentation — the same people, with the phone tree replaced by something faster — and it was never sold that way. Lesson taken.

The pilot term ended on schedule; the system ran unattended for two more months without a single dispatch error before it was deliberately wound down and its member data deleted.

The codebase remains — a working reference for calling-sheet generation, bundled SMS dispatch, and legacy-system sync — pending a general-use rebuild.
